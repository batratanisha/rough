# Five things that help when you keep running from your own emotions

Running from a feeling rarely looks like running. It looks like checking your phone, opening the fridge, suddenly needing to tidy your desk. There’s a reason those are the classics: people now unlock their phones something like eighty times a day on average, and researchers who study emotional eating find that more than half of stressed adults do a version of it too. We’ve taken flight and dressed it up as errands. Something lands badly, a message, a comment in a meeting, and within seconds you are somewhere else. It can take years to see this as avoidance rather than busyness. Psychologists have a name for it, experiential avoidance, now considered a maintenance factor across an unsettling range of anxiety and mood disorders. And it takes longer still to learn what to do instead. These five things actually help.

## 1. Learn to catch the exit, not the emotion

Trying to notice your feelings directly tends to fail, and there’s a decent reason why. Wendy Wood, who has spent her career at USC studying how habits actually work, found that something like 43 percent of what people do in a day isn’t decided at all. It’s cued. The body reaches for the familiar thing while the mind is busy somewhere else entirely, which is exactly the problem. The urge to leave the room doesn’t wait for you to notice you’re upset. It’s already running on its own circuit, triggered by the context, not by a conscious read of what you’re feeling. By the time a feeling is big enough to notice head on, you have already left.

It works far better to watch for your escape routes instead. The sudden urge to scroll, or the trip to the kitchen when you are not hungry. Everyone has three or four favorite exits, and they are much easier to spot than the emotion underneath them. Once you know yours, treat the reflex as a doorbell. When you catch your hand reaching for the phone without having decided anything, something has arrived, and you are about to pretend you are not home.

## 2. Put the story down and go find the sensation

The moment you do stay, your mind will offer you the story instead: replaying the conversation, drafting the comeback, building your case. The story feels like processing, but it is really another exit, just a more respectable one. A psychologist named Philippe Verduyn, based at a university in Belgium, spent years having people log their emotions as they actually happened, and found that two things decided how long a feeling stuck around more than anything else: how big the moment felt, and how much you replayed it afterward. The first one you don’t control. The second one is just a habit, which means it’s the one worth breaking.

What helps instead is a boring question: where is this in the body? Criticism might look like stiffness under the collarbone and anxiety as churning in the stomach. The location matters less than the looking. This sense even has a name, interoception, and its own dedicated stretch of brain, the insula, that lights up when you do it. There’s good evidence it can be trained, a bit like a muscle. Closing your eyes makes it easier, because with the room gone, whatever is happening inside feels closer, the way sounds feel closer in the dark. The rule is simple. Every time you notice you are back in the story, return to the sensation. Expect to do this ten times in two minutes. That is normal, and it still counts.

## 3. Stay for the length of a song

Here’s the part that actually surprises people: any single wave of feeling, left alone, does not last nearly as long as it feels like it will while you’re in it. You’ve probably heard the claim that emotions only last ninety seconds. It’s a tidy number, and it’s not quite right. When Verduyn had people track full emotional episodes rather than single moments, the range was enormous. Shame and fear tend to burn out in about half an hour, guilt can hang around for three or four hours, and sadness, given the chance, can take the better part of a week. But a single wave inside any of that, the specific spike you feel right after the bad meeting, the one that has you halfway to the fridge, behaves much more like what addiction researchers call ‘urge surfing.’ It peaks and starts to ease within a few minutes, almost every time, provided you stop feeding it a story. The whole feeling might take a week to move through. This particular wave of it won’t.

So give the wave roughly the length of a song. Sit still, keep your attention on the body, and when the urge to leave shows up, and it always shows up, let it pass without obeying it. The urge to leave is not a sign you are doing it wrong. Noticing it and staying anyway is the entire exercise.

## 4. Afterward, say what it was

When the wave has passed, name what it actually was, in plain words, out loud or on paper. Not ‘I was being stupid about that email’ but ‘I felt hurt, and under the hurt I was scared it meant she is losing respect for me.’ The first phrasing is a verdict. The second is information.

This step is easy to skip because it feels like the real emotional homework, the part that isn’t just sitting there. But naming does something real, and it’s been sitting in brain scanners since 2007. UCLA researchers found that when people put a feeling into words, activity drops almost immediately in the amygdala, the part of the brain that runs the alarm, even though nothing about the situation has changed except the sentence describing it. Naming isn’t just a nice metaphor for calming down. It appears to be an actual mechanism for it, routed through a completely different part of the brain than the one that was panicking. The next time that feeling arrives, it arrives with a label already on it, and a labeled feeling is far less frightening than an anonymous one.

## 5. Know which feelings not to face alone

There are limits to all of this, and it matters to be honest about them. Staying works for the ordinary weather of a week: irritation, disappointment, loneliness, the sting of criticism. It is not a technique for grief or for anything connected to real trauma. That’s not a caveat added to sound responsible. It’s the actual finding. Every serious trauma treatment is built around a second person for a reason: a nervous system that gets flooded past a certain point doesn’t heal from being left alone with it, it just gets re-flooded, which is its own kind of harm. The avoidance research backs this up from the other direction, too. It isn’t that avoidance itself is the enemy. It’s avoidance as your only setting, applied indiscriminately to things that were never meant to be handled solo, that keeps showing up linked to worse depression, worse anxiety, worse everything, study after study. When a feeling overwhelms you every single time you approach it, the brave move is not to stay longer. It is to stop treating it as a solo project.

None of this will make you a calm person, and that is not the goal. Most nights the exit will still win, because it’s easy. But what’s important to know is that a feeling costs two minutes when you stay with it, whereas avoiding it costs a whole evening of low-grade static. The research on emotional suppression backs that up. People told to simply hold a feeling in, rather than express or work through it, reliably do worse afterward on memory and attention. The bill still comes. It just arrives disguised as being tired for no reason.

Somewhere along the way you stop mistaking composure for resilience. Composure can just be fast running, and fast running takes fuel, even when it looks from the outside like standing still. The sturdier thing looks unimpressive: a person sitting in a chair, eyes closed, feeling something they would rather not feel, and not leaving. It is a smaller achievement than it sounds, and also a bigger one.
